The slow return of the night train

By Ines Halvorsen · 9 min read · 4 October 2026

At 21:42 the platform at Wien Hauptbahnhof smells of brake dust and oranges. A conductor in a navy waistcoat checks names against a clipboard, and nobody hurries, because the whole point of this train is that you sleep through the hurrying.

Ten years ago most of Europe's sleeper routes were being quietly retired. They were costly to run, awkward to timetable and easy to undercut with a budget flight. Now operators are ordering carriages faster than the factories in Kraków and Ulm can build them.

"You lose an evening and gain a morning. Most people decide that's a fair trade."

The economics turn on one stubborn idea: a bed is worth more than a seat. A couchette sold twice a night, outbound and back, earns more than a daytime carriage that sits half-empty after the morning rush.

Somewhere past Linz the corridor lights dim to amber. A boy in the next compartment is reading under a blanket with a torch. The window shows nothing but your own reflection and, now and then, a level crossing ringing for no one.

By the time the train noses into Venezia Santa Lucia the coffee is terrible and nobody minds.
